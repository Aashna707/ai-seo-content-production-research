# AI-Powered SEO Content Production
## A Research-Based Playbook & SOP

**Based on:** Research across 10 experts, 40+ LinkedIn posts, and 12 YouTube videos

**Central Question:** How are leading SEO experts using AI to create, optimize, scale, and improve content?

---

## Why I Wrote This Playbook

When I started this project, I thought I was researching AI writing.

A few days later, I realized I wasn't.

Every expert I studied was talking about AI, but almost none of them were actually obsessed with writing faster. They were talking about something much bigger. Search itself is changing.

Google no longer behaves the way it did a few years ago. AI Overviews have changed how people interact with search results. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and other answer engines have changed how people discover information. Traditional SEO metrics are becoming less reliable, and content that once performed well isn't automatically visible anymore.

At first, all of these changes felt disconnected.

Rand Fishkin was publishing research on zero-click searches.

Mike King was explaining retrieval systems and AI search mechanics.

Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR was diving into semantic SEO and topical authority.

Lily Ray kept bringing every discussion back to Google's quality systems and E-E-A-T.

Kevin Indig was looking at the business implications for enterprise SEO.

Ross Simmonds focused on distribution.

Jason Barnard talked about entities.

Nathan Gotch built practical production systems.

Aleyda Solís concentrated on implementation.

Chris Donnelly explored automation.

Initially, I treated these as ten separate perspectives.

The more I read, the more I realized they were describing different parts of the same system.

That became the turning point for this research.

Instead of collecting advice from ten experts, I started comparing how their ideas connected. Whenever multiple experts, coming from completely different backgrounds, arrived at the same conclusion, I paid much closer attention. Those recurring patterns felt far more valuable than any single opinion.

That's what shaped this playbook.

This isn't intended to be another collection of AI prompts or SEO tactics.

It's my attempt to organize everything I learned into one practical operating system for AI-assisted content production. Every recommendation here is built by comparing experts against each other rather than treating any individual opinion as absolute.

Whenever I offer my own conclusion, it's because I reached it after finding the same idea repeated across multiple independent sources.

# 1. The Strategic Foundation

Before I wrote a single workflow or thought about prompts, I kept coming back to one question.

If search itself is changing, should we still build content using the assumptions that worked five years ago?

By the time I'd finished comparing all ten experts, my answer was no.

Interestingly, nobody arrived at that conclusion in exactly the same way.

Rand Fishkin approached it through search behavior.

Mike King explained it through retrieval systems.

Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR reached it through semantic relationships.

Kevin Indig looked at it through enterprise strategy.

Lily Ray framed it through Google's quality systems.

Different starting points. Remarkably similar destination.

That gave me much more confidence that this wasn't another temporary industry trend. It felt like multiple people describing the same shift from different angles.

Before worrying about production speed, AI prompts, automation, or content scaling, I think every team needs to rethink three assumptions that traditional SEO has relied on for years.

Everything else in this playbook builds on those three ideas.

## 1.1 Traffic Is No Longer the Best Measure of Success

One of the biggest assumptions I had before starting this research was that SEO success and traffic growth naturally moved together.

That assumption became much harder to defend after reading Rand Fishkin's research.

According to SparkToro and Datos, zero-click searches now account for **27.2% of US searches**, and the trend continues moving upward. At the same time, AI Overviews now appear in roughly one out of every five Google searches. Even more striking, when users are presented with an AI Overview, external websites receive a click only around **1% of the time**.

*(Source: Rand Fishkin, SparkToro/Datos State of Search Report, November 2025 —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/randfishkin_if-you-somehow-missed-our-joint-report-with-activity-7395248123226460160-cdF1)*

I remember reading those numbers twice because my first reaction was that they sounded catastrophic for SEO.

They aren't.

They're catastrophic for outdated SEO reporting.

That distinction matters.

When I compared Fishkin's research with Kevin Indig's work, I stopped looking at traffic decline as evidence that SEO was becoming less valuable. Instead, I started looking at it as evidence that users were completing more of their research before ever clicking through to a website.

That completely changes what content teams should be measuring.

Instead of asking whether an article generated more sessions than last month, the more useful questions have become:

- Are AI systems citing our content?
- Is our brand appearing more frequently inside AI-generated answers?
- Are branded searches increasing because people discovered us through AI?
- When AI does send visitors to our site, are those visitors converting better than traditional organic traffic?

Those metrics tell a much richer story than traffic alone. They also align much more closely with how people are actually searching today.

Something else became obvious while thinking through this shift. Most stakeholders haven't made it yet. If an SEO team introduces AI-first content production without first changing how success is measured, they'll almost certainly find themselves defending declining traffic numbers, even if brand visibility, AI citations, and qualified leads are steadily increasing.

The reporting has to evolve alongside the strategy. Otherwise the strategy will appear unsuccessful using metrics it was never designed to optimize.

## 1.2 Build a Topical Map Before Writing Anything

The second idea that kept resurfacing throughout my research wasn't about AI at all.

It was about coverage. Not keyword coverage. Topic coverage.

I found this particularly interesting because Mike King and Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR approach the problem from completely different directions, yet they arrive at almost the same recommendation.

Mike King explains Google's AI Mode through **query fan-out**.

Instead of treating a search as one isolated question, AI expands it into a network of related questions, often somewhere between **20 and 50 sub-queries**, before deciding what information deserves to be included in the final answer.

*(Source: Mike King, iPullRank, AI Search Manual Walkthrough, September 2025 —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ukpU-EfRtV4)*

When I first read that, I immediately started thinking about content differently. For years, SEO conversations have revolved around choosing the right keyword. But if Google's AI systems are actually evaluating an entire neighborhood of related questions, then writing one excellent article is no longer enough. The article has to exist inside an ecosystem of supporting knowledge.

That's where Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR's work suddenly clicked for me.

Koray rarely talks about query fan-out. Instead, he explains how search engines develop confidence in a website by recognizing comprehensive topical authority.

At first, I thought those were two separate ideas. After spending more time with both experts, I don't think they are.

Mike is explaining **how AI retrieves information**.

Koray is explaining **why one source becomes more trustworthy than another**.

They're describing different parts of the same system.

*(Source: Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR, Holistic SEO & Digital —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/koray-tugberk-gubur_semantic-seo-course-announcement-ask-questions-activity-7057792993763753986-KQfn)*

That realization completely changed how I think about content planning.

If AI systems expand every important query into dozens of connected questions, then the planning process can't start with a title. It has to start with a map.

Before writing anything, I would define the complete topic landscape I want the website to own. Not just the main keyword. Everything surrounding it — the related questions, the supporting concepts, the entities, the objections, the comparisons, the beginner topics, the advanced topics, the follow-up questions people naturally ask after reading the first article.

Only once that map exists would I start deciding which individual articles deserve to be written first.

Without that map, content production becomes reactive. One week you're writing about Topic A because keyword volume looks attractive. The next week you're writing Topic B because a competitor published something similar. Eventually you accumulate dozens of articles, but they don't necessarily strengthen one another because they were never planned as one connected body of knowledge.

The practical process I'd follow looks something like this:

1. Define the primary topic the business wants to become known for.
2. Use tools such as Qforia (ipullrank.com/tools/qforia) to understand how AI expands that topic into related searches.
3. Organize those searches into logical clusters rather than isolated keywords.
4. Identify the gaps where important questions remain unanswered.
5. Prioritize content based not only on search demand but also on how much it strengthens overall topical coverage.

By the time I finished reading Mike King's work alongside Koray's, I stopped seeing topical authority as an advanced SEO tactic. I started seeing it as the foundation that every AI-assisted content strategy should be built on.

## 1.3 Make Information Gain the Standard Every Brief Must Meet

Out of every idea I encountered during this research, this one appeared with the greatest consistency.

Interestingly, almost nobody uses exactly the same language.

Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR calls it **information gain**.

Kevin Indig talks about **defensible content**.

Mike King's research into Google's AI patents points toward retrieval systems rewarding passages that contribute something new instead of repeating what already exists.

Nathan Gotch operationalizes the same idea by encouraging teams to study competitors first and deliberately introduce something they don't have.

Different vocabulary. Same underlying principle.

That was one of the moments where I became much more confident that I wasn't looking at isolated opinions anymore. I was looking at a pattern.

Initially, I assumed AI would make producing content easier. After comparing these experts, I think AI is actually making originality more important.

When generating an article takes only a few minutes, the difficult part is no longer creating words. The difficult part is contributing something worth reading.

Most briefs still begin with things like: what keyword are we targeting, what's the search volume, who ranks today, how many words should we write. Those questions still matter. They're just no longer the first questions I'd ask.

The first question I'd ask is much simpler.

**What new value is this article going to create?**

If nobody can answer that confidently, I don't think the article is ready to be written.

Sometimes that value comes from original research. Sometimes it comes from internal customer data. Sometimes it's first-hand experience. Sometimes it's connecting ideas from multiple disciplines. Sometimes it's simply explaining a complicated concept more clearly than anyone else has.

The format isn't what matters. The contribution is.

*(Source: Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/koray-tugberk-gubur_seo-semanticseo-topicalauthority-activity-7170080710693699584-fk6_)*

*(Confirmed independently by: Mike King, patent analysis —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pQLivtcqCZs)*

Looking back now, I think I underestimated this concept at the beginning of my research. At first it sounded like another SEO buzzword. By the end, after seeing the same idea emerge independently across Koray's semantic SEO work, Mike King's patent analysis, Kevin Indig's strategy, and Nathan Gotch's production process, I stopped treating information gain as another optimization tactic. I started treating it as the quality standard that modern AI search seems to be converging toward.

Instead of asking, *"Can AI write this article?"* I think the better question is:

**"What can our team contribute that AI couldn't possibly know before this article exists?"**

Everything else in the production process becomes much easier once that question has a clear answer.

# 2. Step-by-Step Content Production SOP

Once those three strategic ideas were clear, the production workflow almost built itself.

What surprised me most during this research wasn't how differently the experts approached AI. It was how often they agreed.

Despite all the headlines about AI replacing writers, I couldn't find anyone I genuinely respected arguing for a completely autonomous content operation. Nobody was saying: "Let AI do everything."

Instead, the conversation was consistently about division of responsibility.

AI should accelerate the work it's naturally good at. Humans should spend more time doing the work AI still struggles to replicate.

Kevin Indig arrives at that conclusion through strategy. Lily Ray reaches it through Google's quality systems. Nathan Gotch demonstrates it through practical workflows. Aleyda Solís approaches it from implementation. Ross Simmonds reinforces it through distribution. Even Chris Donnelly, whose work leans most heavily toward automation, still relies on structured human decision-making throughout his workflows.

That consistency gave me confidence that the production process shouldn't revolve around replacing people. It should revolve around using each participant for the type of work they're best suited to perform.

---

## Step 1 — Keyword and Competitor Research (AI-Assisted)

The goal isn't to let AI do the thinking. The goal is to remove the repetitive work so I can spend more time thinking.

That distinction became clearer the more I compared Nathan Gotch's workflow with Chris Donnelly's prompting strategies. Both rely heavily on AI during the research stage, but neither uses AI to decide the strategy. The strategic decisions still come from the person running the process.

Nathan Gotch's workflow is a good example of this. Rather than asking ChatGPT to "write an SEO article," he starts by using AI to analyze competing pages, identify recurring NLP phrases, compare topical coverage, and organize the information into something that's actually useful during planning.

**Prompt framework (R-T-F):**

> "Act as an SEO content strategist. Analyze the following three URLs and extract the top 50 NLP keyword phrases with relevance scores and frequency counts. Format as a table."

*(Source: Nathan Gotch, Gotch SEO —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/nathangotch_how-i-create-seo-content-that-ranks-with-activity-7077989962096259072-HHxF)*

*(Prompt framework: Chris Donnelly —
https://uk.linkedin.com/in/donnellychris)*

Instead of beginning the project with content generation, the workflow begins with understanding. That's exactly where AI creates the most value.

Reading three competing articles line by line isn't intellectually difficult. It's time-consuming. Extracting repeated terminology isn't difficult. It's repetitive. Those are exactly the kinds of tasks AI should handle — they free the content strategist to spend more time making decisions instead of collecting information.

Questions I'd now ask AI during competitor analysis:

- Which topics appear consistently across every ranking page?
- Which entities are mentioned repeatedly?
- Which questions are answered well, and which receive only superficial coverage?
- Where do competing articles contradict one another?
- What information appears to be completely missing?

Those answers are far more valuable than another keyword list. Keywords tell me what competitors are talking about. Those questions tell me where opportunities exist to create something genuinely better.

By the time this step is complete, I don't want AI telling me what article to write. I want it helping me understand what article **still needs to be written.**

## Step 2 — Content Brief Design

If there was one stage that became more important after AI entered content production, it's the brief.

Ironically, AI has made drafting so fast that the quality of the final article now depends much more heavily on everything that happens before the first word is generated.

Nathan Gotch emphasizes structured preparation before drafting. Aleyda Solís repeatedly talks about designing workflows instead of chasing tools. Kevin Indig argues that strategy has become the scarce resource now that generating content is inexpensive.

I don't see the brief as an administrative document anymore. I see it as the strategic blueprint for the article. By the time a writer or an AI model receives it, the difficult decisions should already have been made.

Every brief in this workflow includes the following elements:

| Brief Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Target query + query fan-out sub-queries | Covers the complete semantic neighborhood rather than one keyword |
| Top 50 NLP keywords | Reflects language competitors consistently use |
| Competitor heading structure | Establishes the minimum coverage readers already expect |
| Information gain requirement | Forces the team to define what this article contributes that competitors don't |
| Entity list | Ensures important people, brands, products, and concepts appear consistently |
| Experience layer specification | Identifies exactly what human expertise must be added later |
| Schema markup type | Determines the structured data required before publication |
| Distribution targets | Prevents distribution from becoming an afterthought |

*(Sources: Aleyda Solís —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/aleyda_the-new-learningaisearchcom-site-is-activity-7349351328738938881-KWy4;
Nathan Gotch —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wuHUDm4-WE)*

The part I value most is the **information gain requirement**. Not because it's another field inside the brief. Because it changes the conversation before anyone starts writing.

Instead of asking, *"Can we produce this article?"* the team begins asking, *"Should this article exist?"*

That's a much harder question. It's also a much better one.

If nobody can clearly explain what value the article will add beyond what's already available, I don't think the project should move into production. AI has made creating content incredibly easy. That makes choosing **what not to create** even more important.

## Step 3 — AI Draft Generation

This is probably the stage most people expect AI to dominate. Ironically, I think it's the stage where AI should make the fewest important decisions.

By the time drafting begins, the strategy should already be finished. The topic has been chosen. The research has been completed. Competitors have been analyzed. The topical map exists. The entities are defined. The information gain has been identified. The human contribution has already been planned.

At that point, generating the first version of the article becomes largely an organizational task. That's where AI excels. Its responsibility isn't to invent expertise. Its responsibility is to organize expertise.

A good first draft should establish the heading hierarchy, arrange ideas logically, integrate required terminology naturally, and create something that's easy for a human expert to improve.

One idea from Mike King's research completely changed how I think about AI-generated drafts. It wasn't about prompting or models. It was about **retrieval**.

Modern retrieval systems don't necessarily pull entire pages into an answer. More often, they retrieve individual passages that appear to answer a specific question particularly well.

Instead of treating every paragraph as part of one continuous essay, every important section should be able to stand on its own. If Google extracted only one paragraph from the middle of the article, that paragraph should still make complete sense. The reader shouldn't need three earlier sections to understand what it's saying.

*(Source: Mike King, AI Search Manual Walkthrough —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ukpU-EfRtV4)*

Rather than asking, *"Does this paragraph flow nicely?"* I'd probably ask something different first.

*"If this paragraph became the only thing an AI system quoted from this page, would it still answer the question clearly?"*

If the answer is no, I'd rewrite it.

Interestingly, writing sections that can stand independently also improves readability for human readers. Readers rarely consume long-form content from beginning to end anymore. They skim, jump between headings, search within pages, and arrive from AI answers that drop them directly into the middle of an article.

The two goals aren't competing nearly as much as I originally expected.

## Step 4 — Human Expertise Injection (The Step That Matters Most)

If someone asked me which stage of this workflow has the greatest impact on long-term success, this is the one I'd choose without hesitation. Not because it's the most technically complex. Because it's the hardest to replace.

When I started researching AI-assisted content production, I assumed the biggest question would be: *"How much of the writing should AI do?"*

By the end of the research, the more interesting question had become: **"Where does human expertise create the most value?"**

Almost every expert answered that question differently.

Lily Ray kept bringing the conversation back to **Experience** within Google's E-E-A-T framework.

Kevin Indig repeatedly contrasted commodity content with defensible content.

Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR focused on information gain.

Nathan Gotch encouraged adding examples and insights competitors couldn't provide.

Ross Simmonds emphasized creating things that are intentionally difficult to replicate.

On the surface, those ideas look unrelated. I don't think they are. They're all describing the same competitive advantage: humans.

That realization completely changed how I think about editing. The real value comes from adding things AI couldn't have created on its own:

- First-hand experience from working on real projects
- Original customer research
- Internal product knowledge
- Lessons learned from failed experiments
- Case studies and proprietary data
- Opinions shaped through actual implementation

Those contributions aren't just interesting. They're extremely difficult for competitors to copy. Anyone using the same language model can generate a technically correct article. They can't generate your team's experience.

That's why Google's addition of the first **E** in **E-E-A-T** makes much more sense now than it did before this research. AI can reproduce expertise surprisingly well. It can't honestly reproduce experience.

*(Source: Lily Ray, SEO Week 2025 keynote —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vtFN9lDciM)*

Kevin Indig's distinction between commodity content and defensible content also became much clearer after connecting it with Lily Ray's work. Commodity content isn't necessarily inaccurate. It's simply replaceable. Defensible content contains something that couldn't have existed before the organization contributed it.

*(Source: Kevin Indig —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kevinindig_ai-content-seo-activity-7137469480791621633-XCxZ)*

The filter I'd use before publication: **What is uniquely ours about this article?**

Sometimes the answer will be a single paragraph. Sometimes it will be an original chart. Sometimes it's one lesson learned after implementing the strategy with customers. The size of the contribution isn't what matters. The originality is.

## Step 5 — Quality Gate

AI hasn't made quality control less important. It's made it more important than ever.

A few years ago, content production naturally limited itself. Research took time. Writing took time. Editing took time. That friction has almost disappeared. Today, a reasonably good first draft can be generated in minutes.

The bottleneck hasn't disappeared. It's moved. We're no longer constrained by how quickly we can produce content. We're constrained by how confidently we can decide that the content deserves to be published.

Nathan Gotch's publishing checklist reinforced this idea. Rather than relying on instinct or individual preferences, every article should pass through the same quality gate before publication.

| Check | Standard |
|---|---|
| Grammar and readability | Grammarly 95+ score |
| Factual accuracy | Every statistic verified against the original source |
| E-E-A-T compliance | Includes documented first-hand experience wherever appropriate |
| Information gain | Clearly contributes something competitors don't |
| Passage extractability | Every important section stands on its own if retrieved by an AI system |
| Entity coverage | Important entities are referenced accurately and consistently |
| Schema markup | Appropriate structured data added before publication |

*(Source: Nathan Gotch, AI SEO for Dummies —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wuHUDm4-WE)*

Most of the items in this checklist have very little to do with AI. They're simply good publishing practices. AI hasn't replaced those standards. If anything, it's exposed how important they always were.

Quality isn't something added during the last hour before publication. It's something built gradually into every stage of the workflow. The quality gate simply confirms that those earlier decisions were made well.

## Step 6 — Technical Publishing Requirements

By the time an article reaches this stage, most of the difficult thinking should already be finished. But technical publishing isn't the most exciting part of content production, and after reading Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR's work alongside Mike King and Jason Barnard, I came away with a much greater appreciation for why it matters.

Search engines don't experience content the way humans do. People notice arguments, stories, examples. Search systems notice structure, relationships, entities, schema, context. Those two perspectives need to work together.

At a minimum, every article should include:

- Appropriate schema markup such as Article, FAQ, HowTo, or Organization where relevant
- A logical H1 → H2 → H3 hierarchy that reflects the actual structure of the content
- Submission through Google Search Console so indexing begins as quickly as possible
- Meaningful internal links that genuinely help readers continue exploring the topic
- A scheduled review date so important content doesn't quietly become outdated

*(Source: Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/koray-tugberk-gubur_semantic-seo-the-future-of-seo-activity-7104841441536806912-ZylR)*

The scheduled review was probably the recommendation I underestimated most. Before this project, I unconsciously treated publishing as the finish line.

After comparing these experts, I don't think publishing is the end of the workflow. It's the beginning of another one. Search behaviour changes. AI systems evolve. Competitors publish better research. Statistics become outdated.

The strongest content assets behave more like products than blog posts. Products receive updates. Content deserves the same mindset. If an article becomes strategically important for the business, someone should continue owning it after publication — not because something is wrong, but because great content compounds when it's continuously improved instead of abandoned.

# 3. GEO Optimization Layer

When I first came across the term **Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)**, I assumed it was another marketing buzzword trying to replace SEO with a new acronym.

After working through the research, I don't think that's what GEO is at all.

None of the experts I studied argue that traditional SEO has become irrelevant. If anything, they assume good SEO is already in place. GEO simply builds on top of it.

AI systems still need high-quality information. They still need trusted sources. They still rely on authority, entities, structure, and relevance. Those are all things traditional SEO has always tried to improve.

The difference is what happens **after** that information becomes discoverable.

Traditional SEO is primarily concerned with helping users find your page. GEO is concerned with helping AI systems understand, retrieve, trust, and cite the right parts of that page.

Instead of asking, *"How do I rank this page?"* I found myself asking, *"If an AI system needed to answer this question in twenty seconds, what part of my page would it choose?"*

That question immediately shifts the focus from pages to passages, from rankings to retrieval, from visibility to usefulness.

---

## 3.1 Reverse-Engineer AI Overview Citations

Instead of trying to guess why Google's AI Overview selected a particular source, start by studying the answer Google already produced.

Tools such as Profound and ZipTie make this much easier because they identify which passages Google highlights inside AI-generated answers.

Once those passages are visible, the question changes completely. Instead of asking, *"Why didn't we rank?"* we can ask, *"What made this specific paragraph more useful than ours?"*

*(Source: Lily Ray, SEO Week 2025 —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vtFN9lDciM)*

If Google consistently retrieves one competitor's paragraph, there's probably a reason. Maybe it answers the question immediately. Maybe it explains the concept more clearly. Maybe it includes first-hand data. Maybe it simply removes ambiguity better than competing pages. Whatever the reason, it's observable. That makes optimization much more objective.

One thing I'd avoid is copying the cited passage. The goal isn't imitation. The goal is understanding. Studying retrieved passages also creates a feedback loop that most SEO workflows never had before — content teams can begin learning which types of explanations, structures, examples, and evidence AI systems consistently choose to surface.

---

## 3.2 Structure Content for Retrieval

The passages AI systems consistently cite usually share several characteristics. They're focused. They're direct. They answer one question well. They don't require three paragraphs of context before reaching the point.

Because of that, I'd structure every article around independently useful sections.

- Each major heading should answer a specific question
- The first sentence beneath that heading should immediately address the topic
- Include specific numbers, dates, percentages, and named sources
- Supporting evidence, examples, and nuance can follow afterwards, but the reader shouldn't need to search for the answer

This naturally reduces one of the biggest weaknesses in AI-generated content. Many AI drafts begin with long introductions before actually answering the question. If another source answers the question immediately, that's probably the passage that gets selected.

Say the important thing first. Support it second. Expand it afterwards.

## 3.3 Build Entity Signals Before You Need Them

Jason Barnard completely changed the way I think about brands online.

Instead of viewing a company website as the centre of everything, he explains that AI systems gradually build confidence by connecting information across many different trusted sources. Your website is only one piece of that puzzle. Your LinkedIn profile contributes another signal. Industry directories contribute another. Podcast appearances. Conference speaker pages. Guest articles. Company profiles. Media coverage.

None of those sources tell the complete story on their own. Together, they help search engines and AI systems build confidence that a particular person, company, or organisation genuinely exists and can be understood consistently.

*(Source: Jason Barnard, Kalicube —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jasonmbarnard_why-knowledge-panels-are-key-to-your-strategy-activity-7091553395274080256-s0nc)*

The real objective isn't appearing on one authoritative website. It's creating a consistent identity across the entire web. If every trustworthy source describes your company differently, AI systems receive mixed signals. If every trustworthy source reinforces the same identity, expertise, products, and positioning, confidence naturally increases.

Entity optimisation feels much closer to brand consistency than traditional technical SEO. It's less about gaming algorithms. It's more about removing ambiguity.

---

## 3.4 Publish Beyond Your Website: Why YouTube and Transcripts Matter

The strongest content rarely exists in just one place.

Lily Ray points out that YouTube has become one of the most frequently cited sources inside AI-generated answers. When those videos are supported with complete transcripts, they become searchable in multiple ways simultaneously.

The video can appear inside YouTube. The transcript can appear inside Google Search. Both become candidates for retrieval inside AI-generated answers.

*(Source: Lily Ray —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UDexUcyPC04)*

I no longer see a blog post and a YouTube video as two separate pieces of content. I see them as two different ways of expressing the same expertise. Both expand the number of places where AI systems can encounter, understand, and retrieve the underlying ideas.

Every format reaches a different audience. Every format strengthens the underlying entity. Every format creates another opportunity for AI retrieval. Once I started thinking about content this way, repurposing stopped feeling like recycling. It became amplification.

# 4. Distribution Strategy

Before reading Ross Simmonds' work, I treated distribution as something that happened after content production. The article gets published. Someone shares it on LinkedIn. Maybe it gets posted on X. Then everyone moves on to the next article.

Looking back, that workflow feels backwards.

Ross Simmonds repeatedly argues that distribution isn't something you add after content is finished. It should influence the content from the very beginning.

*(Source: Ross Simmonds, Zero Click Conference —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VXxFJAg7YJw)*

This also connects surprisingly well with Rand Fishkin's research. Fishkin argues that AI systems increasingly learn from authoritative sources across the web. Jason Barnard explains how authoritative mentions strengthen entity understanding. Ross Simmonds focuses on systematically placing valuable ideas in those authoritative environments.

Three different experts. Three different specialties. One shared conclusion.

Publishing excellent content on your own website isn't enough. People need to encounter it elsewhere too. Distribution has become part of SEO itself. Every credible mention outside your own website strengthens the network of information AI systems use to understand who you are and why your content deserves to be trusted.

---

## Distribution Should Begin Before the Article Exists

If I already know where I want this research to appear, shouldn't that influence how I write it?

I think the answer is yes.

That's why I'd build a distribution plan into the content brief rather than creating it after publication. By the time the article reaches editing, the team should already know which publications are worth approaching, which communities might find the research genuinely useful, which creators or industry experts could realistically reference it, and which formats should be produced alongside the article.

---

## Priority Distribution Targets

### 1. Major Editorial Publications

Authoritative publications create several benefits at the same time. They expose the research to new audiences. They strengthen brand credibility. They reinforce entity signals. They also increase the chances that future AI systems encounter your work through trusted third-party sources rather than only through your own website.

### 2. Reddit Communities

Reddit appeared repeatedly in AI-generated answers across multiple topics. That isn't happening by accident. People use Reddit to discuss practical experiences, recommendations, failures, comparisons, and opinions that don't always appear in polished marketing content. AI systems seem to value those conversations.

The value comes from contributing genuinely useful expertise rather than trying to insert links into unrelated discussions.

### 3. YouTube

Publishing videos alongside transcripts creates another searchable representation of the same expertise. Different users naturally search in different environments. One strong idea expressed across multiple formats has far more opportunities to be discovered than one excellent article sitting quietly on a company blog.

### 4. Industry Directories and Databases

Directories help reinforce entity consistency. When AI systems repeatedly encounter the same company information across trustworthy sources, confidence naturally increases. That makes directories much more valuable than their traffic numbers alone might suggest.

### 5. Podcasts

Podcasts create something written content can't easily replicate: conversation. They're often where founders, operators, consultants, and practitioners explain why they made particular decisions rather than simply documenting what they did. One appearance can quietly create value across multiple surfaces at the same time.

---

**The "Create Once, Distribute Forever" rule:** Every major piece of original research should be distributed to a minimum of 10 authoritative sources. Each distribution touchpoint is a separate AI citation opportunity.

*(Source: Ross Simmonds —
https://ca.linkedin.com/in/rosssimmonds)*

Great research shouldn't disappear after publication. It should continue creating value for months, sometimes years, through thoughtful distribution into places where new audiences and AI systems can continue discovering it. That's a much bigger ambition than promoting a blog post. It's about building a durable presence that extends far beyond your own website.

# 5. Measurement Framework

Creating better content is only half the challenge. Knowing whether that content is actually succeeding inside AI-driven search experiences is becoming just as important.

For years, SEO reporting has revolved around three familiar metrics: organic traffic, keyword rankings, and click-through rate. Those metrics aren't wrong. They're just becoming incomplete.

Rand Fishkin's zero-click research shows why. If users increasingly receive useful answers before clicking through to a website, then traffic naturally declines even when visibility increases.

Kevin Indig arrives at a similar conclusion from a different direction. He argues that the remaining visitors often represent much stronger buying intent because AI has already answered many of their early research questions before they arrive.

A decline in sessions doesn't automatically mean a decline in impact. Sometimes it means users are interacting with your brand earlier in the journey through AI-generated answers before visiting later with much stronger intent.

---

## The Metrics I'd Stop Prioritizing

| Traditional Metric | Why It Becomes Less Reliable |
|---|---|
| Organic traffic | AI increasingly satisfies informational searches before users click through |
| Keyword rankings | AI Overviews often appear above traditional organic listings |
| SERP click-through rate | As AI-generated answers become more useful, fewer users need to click even when your information influences their decision |

---

## The Metrics I'd Start Measuring Instead

| AI-Era Metric | How I'd Measure It |
|---|---|
| AI citation frequency | Track with Profound, ZipTie, or structured manual testing across AI platforms |
| Brand mentions inside AI-generated answers | Separate linked mentions from unlinked mentions |
| Share of voice across AI search | Test a fixed group of important prompts across Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini every month |
| AI-referred conversion rate | Segment AI-originated traffic inside GA4 and compare conversion behaviour with traditional organic visitors |
| Branded search growth | Monitor Google Search Console and Google Trends for increases in brand awareness over time |
| Entity clarity | Regularly test prompts such as *"Who is [Brand]?"* across major AI platforms |

*(Source: Kevin Indig —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_jVxehRG1w;
Aleyda Solís —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/aleyda_seo-for-2025-1-create-original-content-activity-7278392661948964864-CzjN)*

I particularly like measuring entity clarity because it shifts the conversation away from rankings and toward understanding. If four different AI systems describe your company in four completely different ways, that probably indicates a much deeper branding problem than simply losing one keyword position.

Visibility isn't the end goal. Business outcomes are. Questions like: Are branded searches increasing? Are more prospects mentioning AI tools during sales conversations? Are leads arriving with greater product awareness? Is the sales cycle becoming shorter because AI answered earlier-stage questions? Those answers matter much more than a dashboard full of isolated SEO metrics.

# 6. Where the Experts Disagree

One of the biggest surprises during this research wasn't how often the experts disagreed. It was how often I *thought* they disagreed when they actually didn't.

Many apparent disagreements came from looking at the same problem from different levels. Some experts explain how search systems work. Others focus on how businesses should respond. Others are concerned with how content teams should operate day to day.

The disagreements that remained were genuinely interesting because they reveal where AI-powered SEO is still evolving.

---

## Disagreement 1: Should We Optimize Primarily for Google or for AI-Native Search?

### Kevin Indig's Perspective

Kevin Indig generally treats Google as the centre of the search ecosystem. That isn't because he ignores ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or other AI platforms. It's because Google still processes the overwhelming majority of searches, and AI Overviews are becoming part of Google's own experience rather than replacing it.

*(Source: Kevin Indig —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_jVxehRG1w)*

### Ross Simmonds' Perspective

Ross Simmonds looks at the same landscape through a wider lens. Instead of thinking primarily about Google, he encourages marketers to consider every place where buyers discover information — including ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, YouTube, and communities. His argument isn't that Google has become unimportant. It's that buyers rarely stay inside one platform anymore.

*(Source: Ross Simmonds —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TMHNuhLvLbQ)*

### Where I Landed

I think they're solving different business problems.

If I were responsible for SEO at a consumer brand where Google still dominates discovery, I'd lean much closer to Kevin Indig's approach. If I were working with a B2B SaaS company selling complex products with long buying cycles, Ross Simmonds' perspective becomes much more compelling. Enterprise buyers rarely trust the first answer they find. Their research journey naturally spreads across several platforms.

Rather than asking whether Google or AI-native search deserves priority, I'd start by asking where my customers actually spend their time making decisions.

---

## Disagreement 2: Is Programmatic SEO Finished?

### Kevin Indig's Perspective

Kevin Indig argues that traditional programmatic SEO has lost much of the advantage it once had. AI has dramatically lowered the barrier to producing volume. Scale alone no longer creates differentiation. Quality becomes the scarce resource.

*(Source: Kevin Indig —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kevinindig_seo-programmaticseo-ai-activity-7082354507581444097-2uT8)*

### Nathan Gotch's Perspective

Nathan Gotch doesn't reject programmatic SEO. He updates it. In his workflow, automation still has an important role — but every page now has to contribute genuine value. That means stronger research, human review, information gain, and higher editorial standards.

*(Source: Nathan Gotch —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=06KYHKhSzu0)*

### My Conclusion

Kevin is explaining why the old version stopped working. Nathan is explaining what the next version has to become.

The real question isn't whether programmatic SEO still works. The real question is whether the pages deserve to exist. If every page contributes original insight, useful structure, or information competitors haven't provided, scale becomes a force multiplier. If every page says roughly the same thing as every other page on the internet, AI simply allows teams to produce mediocrity faster.

---

## Disagreement 3: How Much Does Wikipedia Matter for Entity SEO?

### Jason Barnard's Perspective

Barnard argues that marketers often overestimate the importance of individual platforms. Wikipedia can contribute useful signals, but what matters is the overall consistency of the entity across dozens of authoritative sources, not whether one specific website mentions it.

*(Source: Jason Barnard —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jasonmbarnard_why-knowledge-panels-are-key-to-your-strategy-activity-7091553395274080256-s0nc)*

### Rand Fishkin's Perspective

Rand Fishkin repeatedly points to the value of authoritative mentions across trusted publications. Those mentions don't simply influence backlinks or referral traffic. They become part of the broader information ecosystem that search engines and AI systems learn from over time.

*(Source: Rand Fishkin —
https://searchengineland.com/seo-opportunity-shrinking-rand-fishkin-448876)*

### My Conclusion

This disagreement disappeared almost completely once I stopped thinking about Wikipedia as the goal.

Barnard isn't saying Wikipedia lacks value. He's saying it shouldn't become the entire strategy. Fishkin isn't saying authoritative publications replace entity optimization. He's showing why those publications matter. Those ideas reinforce one another.

If I were building an entity strategy today, I wouldn't chase one specific platform. I'd focus on making sure every credible source consistently describes the business in the same way.

# 7. What I Rejected and Why

One decision I made early in this project was that I didn't want to include every recommendation simply because an expert I respected had mentioned it. One of the advantages of studying multiple experts instead of following a single person is that it forces you to make decisions.

---

## Rejected Idea 1: Publish as Much AI Content as Possible to Build Topical Authority Faster

On the surface, it sounds completely logical. If topical authority comes from covering an entire subject, then publishing hundreds or even thousands of AI-generated pages should strengthen topical authority more quickly.

Today, I don't think it does.

The more I compared Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR's research with Lily Ray's observations about Google's Helpful Content systems, the less convincing this strategy became.

Koray repeatedly explains that authority comes from demonstrating deep understanding of a topic, not simply increasing the number of URLs associated with it. Lily Ray reaches a similar conclusion from another direction — Google's quality systems have become increasingly effective at distinguishing genuinely helpful content from pages created primarily to capture search traffic.

*(Sources: Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR —
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/koray-tugberk-gubur_semantic-seo-course-announcement-ask-questions-activity-7057792993763753986-KQfn;
Lily Ray —
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vtFN9lDciM)*

Publishing more content doesn't automatically strengthen authority. Publishing better content does.

The barrier to creating another article has almost disappeared. The barrier to creating another article that's genuinely worth citing hasn't. That's why I deliberately rejected the idea of using AI simply to flood a topic with more pages.

---

## Rejected Idea 2: Fully Autonomous Content Distribution

Chris Donnelly's work introduced some fascinating possibilities around AI agents handling increasingly complex workflows. Technically, I don't think those ideas are unrealistic.

Where I became more cautious was distribution.

The kind of distribution that creates lasting authority isn't primarily operational. It's relational. Getting an original research report published by a respected industry publication usually depends on relationships, editorial judgement, credibility, and trust. Being invited onto a podcast happens because someone believes your perspective is worth hearing.

AI can absolutely support those processes. It can help identify publications, summarise editors' previous work, draft personalised outreach, and recommend follow-up timing. I would happily use AI for all of those tasks.

What I wouldn't automate is the relationship itself. Relationships create authority precisely because they're difficult to manufacture. If every organisation begins sending perfectly written AI-generated outreach at enormous scale, editors will naturally become even more selective. Authenticity becomes more valuable as automation increases.

I'd absolutely automate administration. I wouldn't automate trust.

# 8. My Original Ideas

Throughout the research, I kept asking myself one question. If these experts are all describing different parts of the same system, what happens when those pieces are connected?

One gap stood out more than the others. Everyone talks about AI visibility. Very few people explain how content teams should monitor and improve that visibility month after month.

---

## Original Idea: The Monthly AI Citation Audit

Lily Ray explains how to analyse AI Overview citations. Kevin Indig encourages measuring AI visibility instead of relying entirely on traffic. Ross Simmonds repeatedly argues that content should continue creating value long after publication. Aleyda Solís discusses measuring AI performance across platforms.

Those ideas all exist independently. What I couldn't find was one operational process that combined them into a recurring habit. That's what this audit is designed to do.

### The Workflow

Every month, the content team should:

1. Identify the twenty most important category queries for the business
2. Test those same queries across Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini
3. Record every citation that appears
4. Note whether the brand is mentioned
5. Record which competitors appear consistently
6. Analyse the retrieved passages using tools such as Profound or ZipTie
7. Compare those passages with the company's own content
8. Identify missing entities, weaker explanations, outdated information, or missing evidence
9. Update the content
10. Repeat the process using exactly the same query set the following month

### Why It Could Work

Traditional SEO has rank tracking. Paid acquisition has campaign dashboards. AI search still feels surprisingly informal. Many teams only realise they aren't appearing inside AI-generated answers because someone happens to test a prompt during a meeting. That's not a measurement system. It's luck.

A recurring audit changes that. Instead of occasionally checking AI visibility, the team builds it into the operating rhythm of the content program. Over time, those observations become increasingly valuable. Patterns emerge. Certain competitors appear repeatedly. Some article structures earn citations more consistently than others. Specific entities become associated with particular topics.

If AI systems retrieve passages, and those passages can be inspected, and those passages can be compared, then improving AI visibility shouldn't feel mysterious. It becomes another optimisation process — one that continues for as long as the content remains strategically important.

# 9. Weaknesses of This Playbook

I don't think any research project is complete without acknowledging where it becomes less certain.

---

## Limitation 1: Most of the Evidence Comes from Practitioners Rather Than Controlled Research

Most of the evidence throughout this playbook comes from practitioners. People like Mike King, Lily Ray, Kevin Indig, Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR, Ross Simmonds, Jason Barnard, Nathan Gotch, Aleyda Solís, Rand Fishkin, and Chris Donnelly spend their time testing ideas inside real businesses rather than controlled laboratory environments.

That makes their work extremely practical. It also introduces limitations. Many recommendations are based on observations, case studies, client work, experiments, and repeated implementation rather than peer-reviewed validation.

To reduce that risk, I deliberately placed much greater weight on ideas that appeared independently across several experts. Even then, AI search is still an emerging discipline. Some recommendations in this playbook will almost certainly change as search systems continue evolving.

---

## Limitation 2: AI Search Is Moving Faster Than Documentation Can Keep Up

Google has already introduced multiple changes to AI Overviews since launch. AI Mode continues developing. ChatGPT Search continues improving. What appears to be best practice today may become outdated surprisingly quickly.

I'd treat this playbook much more like an operating manual that deserves regular revision — perhaps a quarterly review where the team revisits major assumptions, checks whether the supporting research still holds, and updates the workflow wherever necessary.

---

## Limitation 3: Smaller Teams May Not Have Access to Every Recommended Tool

Several recommendations assume access to tools that many smaller organizations may not use. Platforms like Profound and ZipTie make AI citation analysis significantly easier. Not every business can justify those costs.

If I were working inside a startup with one content marketer and a limited budget, I'd simplify the workflow rather than abandon it. Instead of monitoring fifty prompts every month, I'd monitor ten. The principles stay the same. Only the scale changes.

---

## Limitation 4: Human Expertise Can't Be Manufactured

Many organizations keep their deepest expertise inside engineering, product, customer success, or consulting teams. Those people aren't always available to contribute to content production at the frequency this playbook requires.

Even short interviews can make an enormous difference. One conversation with a product manager or consultant often produces insights that dozens of AI-generated summaries never could. Successful AI-assisted content production depends just as much on organizational collaboration as it does on good prompts.

---

## Limitation 5: Many of These Strategies Require Patience

Several recommendations throughout this playbook compound slowly. Entity building. Editorial relationships. Original research. Podcast appearances. Brand recognition. Those investments rarely produce immediate results.

Ironically, I think that's exactly what makes them valuable. Anyone can generate another AI-assisted article tomorrow. Far fewer organizations are willing to spend the next twelve months building genuine authority. The harder something is to accelerate with AI, the more likely it is to become a sustainable competitive advantage.

# 10. Who I Would NOT Recommend Following and Why

Choosing someone for this section was much harder than I expected. Every person included in this research influenced the final playbook in some way.

If I had to recommend one expert whose work I'd rely on the least when building an AI SEO content production system, I'd probably choose **Chris Donnelly**.

That isn't a criticism of his expertise. It's a reflection of the type of problems he's solving.

Chris Donnelly produces some of the most useful content I found around prompting, AI workflows, automation, and productivity. Several of his prompting ideas directly influenced parts of this playbook.

Where I think his work differs from the others is focus.

Most of the other experts begin with search. Chris usually begins with AI.

Mike King starts by asking how AI retrieval systems work. Koray starts by asking how search engines understand topics. Lily Ray starts by asking how Google evaluates quality. Kevin Indig starts by asking how businesses should respond strategically. Ross Simmonds starts by asking how expertise reaches the market.

Chris Donnelly often starts by asking how AI can make work faster. That's a valuable question. It just isn't the primary question I wanted this playbook to answer.

If someone asked me where to learn prompting, workflow automation, or AI productivity, I'd happily recommend Chris Donnelly. If someone asked me where to begin understanding how AI is changing SEO itself, I'd recommend spending much more time with Mike King, Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR, Lily Ray, Kevin Indig, Ross Simmonds, Jason Barnard, Nathan Gotch, Aleyda Solís, and Rand Fishkin first.

Then I'd come back to Chris Donnelly to learn how to operationalize those ideas more efficiently.

Learn the strategy first. Then learn how AI can accelerate it.

# Final Reflection

When I started this project, I thought I was going to learn how to produce AI content more efficiently.

Looking back now, I think that assumption was too narrow.

The biggest lesson wasn't about prompting. It wasn't about automation. It wasn't about choosing the right model.

It was realizing that AI is changing the economics of content production.

Creating content has become dramatically easier. Creating content that's genuinely worth retrieving has become dramatically harder.

That's the thread I kept finding regardless of which expert I was reading.

Mike King helped me understand how retrieval works. Koray Tuğberk GÜBÜR changed how I think about topical authority and information gain. Lily Ray kept reminding me that Google's quality systems still matter. Kevin Indig reframed SEO as a business problem rather than simply a ranking problem. Ross Simmonds convinced me that publishing is only the beginning. Jason Barnard fundamentally changed how I think about entities. Nathan Gotch showed how these ideas can become practical workflows. Aleyda Solís demonstrated how they fit inside real production systems. Rand Fishkin challenged the way I interpret traffic. Chris Donnelly highlighted how AI can remove repetitive work without replacing thoughtful decision-making.

Each expert contributed something different. Taken together, though, they all pointed me toward the same conclusion.

AI isn't replacing good content strategy. It's exposing where good content strategy was missing all along.

If there's one idea I hope readers take away from this playbook, it's this:

The future of SEO won't belong to the teams that publish the most content.

It will belong to the teams that consistently create information worth retrieving, worth trusting, and worth citing.

Everything else in this playbook is simply my attempt to build a repeatable system for doing exactly that.
